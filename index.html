In one paragraph

Seva makes local, informal work visible, trusted, and fairly paid.

A customer opens a map and sees verified workers nearby — a maid, an electrician, a physiotherapist, a labourer — and books or calls them directly. A worker registers free in under a minute, is verified by an admin, and becomes discoverable to their whole neighbourhood instead of the handful of people who already have their number.

Seva charges workers nothing: no registration fee, no subscription, and no way to buy a higher position on the map. Position is earned through ratings and completed jobs. The intended revenue is a small commission on finished work, so Seva only earns when a worker does.

The gap

Skills are not the problem. Access is.

India’s informal workforce is enormous and largely invisible to the tools that organise the formal economy. A daily-wage labourer waits at a chowk hoping someone hires him. A skilled maid’s income is capped by how many neighbours happen to have her number.

01

Income that never settles

Irregular work means irregular everything else — rent, school fees, medicine, the ability to plan a month ahead.

02

A workforce that is invisible

Skilled people are unseen not because they lack ability, but because nothing structured connects them to the demand next door.

03

Trust bought on hearsay

Households carry real anxiety about who they let into their home. Word-of-mouth gives no way to judge a stranger.

04

No answer when the usual person is busy

Word-of-mouth works right up until your electrician is on another job. Then there is no second option and no way to compare.

Why it matters

Both problems are the same missing thing: a trusted, structured way to see and choose local help. Solving it is not only a business opportunity. Done well, it raises the floor of dignity and income for people the digital economy usually skips.

What Seva is

Map-first, not form-first.

The core question is spatial — who can help me, near me, now? — so the default screen is a live map of nearby workers. Everything else is secondary to that map.

01

Find the people who are actually near you

Customers open a live map, not a directory. Only workers who are both verified and admin-approved appear on it. Location comes from the device, with a locality fallback so a weak GPS fix never hides someone who should be findable.

02

Judge a stranger before you let them in

The worker card is the single most important screen in the app, because it is where a stranger becomes a candidate. Photo, service, rating, distance, locality, rate, availability, languages spoken, verification badges, and two actions: book, or call.

03

Turn one good job into the next one

A completed, OTP-confirmed job leaves a record. Records become a rating, and a good rating lifts a worker up the map. That is the only currency of visibility on Seva — money never buys rank.

The engine

One loop, and every decision sits somewhere on it.

Seva compounds through a single ring. Break any stage and the wheel stops turning — which is why the roadmap is ordered by which stage is weakest, not by which feature is most exciting.

01

Open

The mark on the splash screen is a portico — four pillars and a roof. Shelter built on structure. That is the promise the rest of the app has to keep.

02

Choose your side

One app, two people: the household that needs help, and the person who does the work. The English / हिन्दी toggle sits here and carries through everything after it.

03

Register in minutes

Name, phone, category, locality, a photo and a government ID. What a worker is asked for depends on their trade — a maid is never asked for a graduation year, and an electrician cannot skip employment history.

04

Pick a service

Thirty-nine categories, each with a Hindi label, plus free text for work that does not fit a box. Local demand does not arrive in tidy taxonomies.

05

See who is nearby

A live map, not a list. Only workers who are verified and admin-approved are placed on it — approval is what makes someone visible, and it can be withdrawn.

06

Check the profile, then book

Rates, distance, languages spoken, availability, rating and verification badges, all before the booking. Overlapping pins fan out so a dense street stays tappable.

07

An OTP starts the job

The customer holds a code; the worker enters it on arrival. A job cannot be marked done without it, which is what makes a completed booking mean something.

08

Agree the price up front

The worker sets their own rate and travel charge. The total is their rate times the hours, plus travel — arithmetic a worker can say out loud and a customer can check.

09

Rate, and it compounds

The rating attaches to the worker and lifts them up the map. Thirty completed jobs become the thing an informal worker has never been able to carry between customers: a record.

Trust & safety

Workers enter homes. Trust cannot be an afterthought.

Signup is fast, but visibility is earned. A worker is never shown to the public until an admin has reviewed and approved them — and approval can be withdrawn the moment it needs to be.

How the safety model works →
Identity on file
A profile photo and a government ID, held privately. ID images are never served from a public bucket.
Human approval
Every worker lands as pending. A person reads the whole application before anyone can see them.
Visibility as a gate
Only approved and verified workers reach the public map. Suspension removes someone immediately.
OTP at job start
Work begins when the customer’s code is entered on site. A job cannot be completed any other way.
Phone numbers stay private
Worker numbers are not readable by the app’s public key. A booking derives the number server-side, for that booking only.
Reputation decides visibility
Ratings, completed jobs and complaints move a worker up or off the map. Payment never does.
Fair pay

The worker sets the price. Not an algorithm.

The whole formula

Total = the worker’s rate × hours worked + their travel charge

Three terms. A worker can explain it out loud, a customer can check it, and a partner can audit it. Rates have a floor — ₹100 an hour, ₹300 a day — so nobody can be listed below a living rate, but the ceiling is entirely theirs.

Deliberately not surge

An algorithm that sets prices takes control away from the worker.

Surge pricing is clever, and it quietly moves price-setting from the person doing the work to the platform. In a city where every customer talks to their neighbours, that is also commercially dangerous. Where demand is high, Seva’s job is to tell the worker so — and let them decide.

Where this goes

One year, three years, ten years.

Each horizon is written as a condition to meet, not a date to hit. A date only tells you when we stopped; a condition tells you whether it worked.

In progress ~1 year

A dense pocket of Varanasi where real customers repeatedly book verified workers. Five to ten completed bookings a day, and proven reliability in at least a few categories.

Still a bet ~3 years

Varanasi-wide liquidity across most core categories, a commission mechanism that actually collects, and a playbook repeatable enough to run in a second city.

The vision ~10 years

A trusted local-work platform across many Indian cities, where any skilled person can be visible and earn with dignity — and anyone who needs help can find it in seconds.
