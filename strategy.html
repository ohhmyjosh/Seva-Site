Seva / Model

Free for workers. Paid only when it works.

Seva takes nothing to exist on the platform and nothing to rank higher. The intended revenue is a small commission on completed jobs — value captured only when value is created. This page explains that, and the one part of it that is not solved.

The model

Commission on completed jobs. Nothing else.

No registration fee, no subscription, no listing fee, no promoted placement. Every one of those charges a worker before they have earned anything — and the workers Seva exists for are precisely the ones who cannot pay in advance.

Never

A fee to exist

Registration is free and stays free. A worker who cannot afford ₹100 today is the whole point of the platform.

Never

A fee to rank

Position on the map is decided by ratings and reliability. There is no sponsored slot, and there never will be.

Only

A share of a finished job

If no work happened, Seva earns nothing. That keeps the incentive pointed at completed, rated, real jobs.

On the numbers you will not find here

It is easy to multiply a worker count by a daily rate and produce an impressive monthly figure. We have done that arithmetic internally and it is a motivational sketch, not a forecast. The unit economics that actually matter — bookings per day, worker reliability, customer repeat rate, and average transaction value — are unknown until the pilot measures them. Everything else is downstream of those four numbers, and we would rather show you them measured than modelled.

The honest problem

A worker paid ₹300 in cash has little reason to remit ₹30 to a platform that gave them the lead for free.

This is the most important unsolved problem in Seva’s economics, and it would be dishonest to present the business model without it. Payment for the pilot is cash. There is no automatic way to collect a commission on cash — which makes commission effectively voluntary until money moves inside the app.

The direction is clear: build an in-app payment path where the commission can be deducted at source, and test workers’ willingness to remit long before the platform depends on it. But that work has to come after the booking flow is proven, not before — otherwise we are building a payment stack for a marketplace that may not have found its market.

What this means for a partner

Seva is not asking anyone to believe the revenue model is solved. The pilot’s job is to prove that people book, that workers stay, and that jobs complete. Monetisation is a second problem, and it only becomes worth solving if the first one is answered.

Competition

The real competitor is not an app. It is word-of-mouth.

In Varanasi, asking a neighbour already works, it is free, and it carries social accountability an app cannot match. Any honest strategy has to start by admitting that.

SituationWord-of-mouthSeva
Your usual person is free Works perfectly. Call them. No advantage. Use your usual person.
Your usual person is busy Dead end, or a round of phone calls. A map of others nearby, right now.
You need someone tonight Depends entirely on who picks up. Urgent availability is visible before you call.
You have never hired this trade No idea who to ask or what to pay. Ratings, verification badges, and a stated rate.
You want to compare Not possible. You get one name. Several nearby workers, side by side.
You are new to the city You have no network to draw on. The network is the map.

The moat is not the feature set

A map, a rating system and a booking flow are all copyable. Any competent team could ship them in a quarter.

What is slow to copy is trust architecture and governance — verification that is actually enforced, a reputation system nobody can buy their way around, and a set of rules about worker treatment that the company has publicly bound itself to. Capability can be matched. Earned trust takes years, and it is lost in one incident.

Go-to-market

Seed density, not spread.

The defining risk of any marketplace is the cold start. A map full of workers is worthless if no customer opens the app, and a customer churns instantly if nobody is available nearby. The two sides have to arrive together, in the same neighbourhood.

The strategic principle

Do not run “get workers” and “get customers” as two separate campaigns across the city. Pick one small locality. Onboard 15–20 workers there. Put QR posters in front of households in that same locality. Local liquidity in one pocket beats thin coverage everywhere — and expansion is then a repeat of that pocket, locality by locality, city by city.

  1. Step 01

    Go where the workers already stand

    A labour chowk or majdoor area in Varanasi. Onboard 10–20 workers in person on day one — names, numbers, categories, localities — registering them by hand where they do not have a smartphone.

  2. Step 02

    Put the demand on the same streets

    QR posters in front of households in that same locality, not across the city. WhatsApp groups, local notices, and word of mouth pointed at the pocket where supply already exists.

  3. Step 03

    Land the first real bookings

    Matched by hand at first if necessary. The first goal is not an elegant funnel; it is proof that a stranger will let a Seva-verified worker into their home and pay them.

  4. Step 04

    Collect ratings and let the wheel turn

    Repeat bookings and worker retention are the signals that matter. Only when the pocket sustains itself does the same loop run in the next one.

On marketing spend

No heavy advertising until the app works, the booking flow works, workers are available, and customers actually book. Early channels are field onboarding, QR posters, WhatsApp groups and worker success stories. Paid ads come later — a small daily test for a week or two, and only once conversion is proven. Money spent before the flywheel turns once is money burned.

Scaling

The founder should build systems that scale, not become the system.

Today one person reviews every registration, handles every complaint, and answers every question. That is correct at this size and fatal at ten times it. The structure that replaces it is already designed — and deliberately not built yet.

The Domain Representative model

Each service category gets an operations lead who is simultaneously a worker advocate for that category. Instead of two thousand workers contacting the founder, communication flows through representatives — so worker numbers grow while founder workload stays roughly flat.

Electricians and physiotherapy, the categories with early traction, are the natural first candidates.

A representative owns onboarding and verification support, day-to-day questions, minor disputes, quality and rating monitoring, worker education, and escalation of serious incidents. The founder steps in only for major disputes, suspected fraud, legal issues and policy changes.

Worker retention
Are workers in this category still active after three months?
Average rating
Is quality holding as the category grows?
Response time
How fast does a worker get an answer?
Customer satisfaction
Do households come back to this category?
Onboarding speed
How long from registration to approved and live?
Dispute count
Rising numbers mean the category needs attention, not the platform.
When it gets built

Only when worker volume justifies the fixed cost, revenue can sustain the salary, and complexity exceeds what one person can manage. Hiring a representative before there is anything to manage adds cost ahead of value — which is why it is designed, dated to a condition, and left on the shelf.

Expansion

The same pocket, again and again.

Network effects in this business are local. More workers in a locality make it more useful to customers there, which attracts more workers there. Nothing about that compounds nationally until it has compounded on one street.

01

Locality by locality

Fifteen Varanasi localities are already mapped in the product. The pilot pocket is one of them; the next is whichever one the evidence points at.

02

Or category by category

Early traction in electricians and physiotherapy suggests the model may also travel by trade — going city-wide in one category before adding another. Which unit is right is an open question the pilot should answer, not a decision to guess now.

03

Then city by city

A second city only makes sense once the first one produced a playbook someone else could run. Local accountability is preserved by the representative and city-operations layers, not abandoned at the border.

Government employment schemes, NGOs and community organisations could all accelerate reach. All of them are unproven for Seva and are treated as experiments rather than plans — and any community rollout runs inside the boundary set out in our non-negotiables: community is a distribution channel, never a gate on who may work.
